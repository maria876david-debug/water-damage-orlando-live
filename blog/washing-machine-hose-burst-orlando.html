Washing Machine Hose Burst in Orlando: What to Do First and How to Stop the Next One

A burst washing machine hose can flood a laundry room in minutes, and the water rarely stops at the laundry room door. Here is what to do first, what drying really involves, and how to stop the next hose from bursting.

MV
Orlando Rapid Restoration Team Local Restoration Experts
• Published: November 12, 2026 • 7 min read • Orlando Technical Team

A washing machine hose is one of the smallest parts of your laundry room, and one of the most destructive when it fails. The two supply hoses behind your washer sit under full household water pressure every hour of every day, even when the machine is off. When one of them splits or pops off its fitting, water keeps flowing until someone turns it off — and in an Orlando home, that water does not stay in the laundry room for long. It runs under baseboards, soaks into drywall, and finds its way to the rooms below or next door.

This guide explains what a burst washing machine hose does to an Orlando home, what to do in the first few minutes, how drying and repair usually work, and how to stop the next hose from bursting. It applies to single-family homes, townhomes, and condos across Downtown Orlando, Winter Park, Lake Nona, College Park, Baldwin Park, and the rest of Orange County.

Why Is a Burst Washing Machine Hose So Damaging?

Most leaks in a home are slow. A dripping pipe or a seeping toilet valve gives you days or weeks to notice the stain. A burst supply hose is different because the water is under pressure and the flow is wide open. A washing machine fills through hoses connected straight to your home's water supply, so a split hose can release a large volume of water in the time it takes to notice the sound, walk to the laundry room, and find the shut-off valve. If the burst happens while you are out, at work, or asleep, the water can run for hours.

Orlando homes add two more problems. First, many laundry rooms sit on the second floor or in a hallway closet above living space, so water from a burst hose does not just wet the laundry room floor — it drips through the ceiling of the room below. Second, Central Florida humidity slows natural drying. A wet subfloor or wall cavity that might dry out on its own in a dry climate stays damp here, and damp drywall and wood are where mold gets its start. Mold can begin growing on wet drywall within 24 to 48 hours in Orlando's humidity, which is why the clock matters more than the size of the puddle you can see.

The other reason a burst hose causes so much damage is that the water reaches places you cannot see. It runs under the washer, behind the baseboards, under vinyl plank or tile, and into the wall cavity behind the laundry room. The visible puddle is usually only part of the story. A proper drying job measures moisture inside walls and under flooring, not just on the surface.

What Should You Do in the First Few Minutes After a Hose Bursts?

Do the simple things first, in this order. Speed matters, but only if you stay safe — never step into standing water near outlets, the washer's power cord, or the dryer, and never touch an electrical fixture that has been in contact with water.

  • Turn off the water at the washer valves: there are usually two small valves on the wall behind the washer, one hot and one cold. Turn both clockwise until they stop. If the valves are stuck or broken, turn off the main water shut-off for the house, usually near the water meter or where the main line enters the home.
  • Turn off power to the laundry area if water has reached outlets or appliances: use the breaker panel, not a wet switch. If you are unsure, stay out of the water and call for help.
  • Move what you can save: lift laundry baskets, detergent, rugs, and anything on the floor out of the water. Move furniture and boxes in adjoining rooms away from the wet wall.
  • Start removing water: towels, a mop, and a wet-dry vacuum will move the standing water. Open the laundry room door and any nearby windows for airflow, but do not run the washer or dryer until the area is dry.
  • Take photos and video before you move things: film the burst hose, the water level, wet walls, and anything damaged, with timestamps. Insurers and contractors both want to see the damage as you found it.
  • Call your insurer the same day: a burst hose is usually treated as a sudden plumbing failure, which is the kind of water damage most homeowners policies are written to cover. Your policy and your deductible decide the rest — read the section on insurance below before you assume anything.

If the water has spread past the laundry room — through a ceiling, into a hallway, or under flooring in the next room — that is the point to call a restoration professional rather than handling it with towels. The drying equipment used after a burst hose, like high-volume air movers and dehumidifiers, is not the same as a household fan, and the moisture readings tell you when a wall is actually dry instead of just looking dry. For a fuller walkthrough of the first day after a major leak, see our guide to the first 24 hours after a burst pipe in Orlando — the steps are the same, because the water behaves the same way.

What Should You Check Once the Water Has Stopped?

Once the valves are closed and the standing water is up, walk the house slowly and check everywhere the water could have travelled. Water follows gravity and the path of least resistance, so the damage is often worst a room away from the laundry.

Check the ceiling and walls of the room below the laundry room for stains, bubbling paint, or sagging drywall. Check the baseboards in the hallway and adjoining rooms — press gently and look for swelling, separation from the wall, or discolouration. Lift the edge of rugs and look underneath. Step on the flooring near the laundry room and feel for soft spots, cupping, or boards that move when they did not before. Open the laundry room closet and look at the back wall, where the hoses connect, for dampness running down behind the washer.

Then check the hose itself. Look at where it failed: a split along the rubber, a bulge near a fitting, a corroded connection, or a hose that simply slipped off because the clamp loosened. Take a photo of the failed hose and keep it — do not throw it away until your insurer has seen it or told you they do not need it. The failed hose is the evidence that the leak was sudden, and it is also the part you need to replace before the washer runs again.

Do not run the washer again until the supply lines are replaced and the laundry area is dry. A second burst on a weakened hose can undo a drying job in minutes.

Rubber Hoses vs Braided Stainless Steel Hoses

Feature Standard rubber hose Braided stainless steel hose
What it is Plain rubber with fabric reinforcement Rubber core wrapped in a braided steel sleeve
How it usually fails Bulges, cracks, and splits as rubber ages Corrosion at the fittings or damage to the braid
Orlando heat and humidity Rubber ages faster in hot, humid laundry rooms The steel sleeve protects the rubber core better
Cost Cheaper per hose Costs more, lasts longer
Best for Short-term or rental use Most Orlando homes, especially second-floor laundry rooms

If your home still has the plain rubber hoses that came with the washer, replacing them with braided stainless steel hoses is the single most useful upgrade in this whole guide. It costs far less than one hour of water damage repair, and it removes the most common failure point in the laundry room.

What Does Drying a Laundry Room Actually Involve?

Real drying after a burst hose is not mopping and opening a window. Water gets into the subfloor, the wall cavities, and the baseboards, and those materials dry slowly because air cannot reach them. A drying job usually starts with moisture readings — a meter pressed against drywall, baseboards, and flooring to map how far the water spread and how wet each material is. Those readings are taken again each day, so the drying stops when the materials reach their normal moisture level, not when the surface feels dry to the touch.

The equipment is straightforward: air movers push dry air across wet surfaces, and dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room's air so the surfaces can keep releasing water. In a laundry room this often means pulling the washer and dryer out, removing wet baseboards, and sometimes cutting a strip of drywall at the bottom of the wall so air can reach the cavity and the back of the baseboard. Flooring may need to come up if water got underneath it — vinyl plank and laminate trap water below them, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the top looks fine.

How long drying takes depends on how much water escaped, which materials got wet, and how quickly the water was stopped. A small burst caught in minutes dries much faster than one that ran while the house was empty. The honest answer is that nobody can give you a drying time until the moisture readings are in hand. If you want a sense of the cost side of the same job, see our guide to how much water extraction costs in Orlando.

One thing worth knowing: drying and repair are two separate jobs. Drying removes the water and stops mold from taking hold. Repair replaces the drywall, baseboards, and flooring that could not be saved. A contractor who skips the drying step and goes straight to new drywall is building over wet material, and the problems — odour, swelling, mould — show up weeks later.

Will Homeowners Insurance Cover a Burst Washing Machine Hose?

In most cases, yes — a burst washing machine hose is the classic example of a sudden and accidental water discharge, which is the kind of water damage a standard Florida homeowners policy is usually written to cover. The key word is sudden. Insurers draw a sharp line between a hose that burst without warning and a hose that had been leaking slowly for months while the wall behind it rotted. The first is usually covered; the second is often treated as a maintenance problem and denied.

That distinction is why the failed hose, your photos, and the date you found the water matter so much. They are what show the insurer the leak was sudden. Keep the hose, keep the photos, and file the claim promptly — most policies ask you to report damage quickly, and delays give an adjuster room to argue the damage grew because you waited.

Your deductible still applies, so a small burst that only wets the laundry room floor may cost less to fix than the deductible itself, in which case filing a claim may not be worth it. Larger bursts — water through a ceiling, soaked subfloors, damaged cabinets — are where the claim earns its keep. For a fuller explanation of how Florida policies treat water damage, see our guide to homeowners insurance and water damage in Florida. How Do You Stop the Next Hose From Bursting?

Prevention here is cheap, simple, and mostly a matter of looking at two hoses once in a while. Put a reminder on your calendar and check them every few months:

  • Replace rubber hoses with braided stainless steel hoses: if your hoses are plain black rubber, replace them. If they are already braided steel, check the fittings for rust or green corrosion and replace the hose if the braid is frayed or kinked.
  • Replace hoses on a schedule, not when they fail: most hose makers suggest replacing supply hoses every few years — check the guidance printed on your own hoses or the maker's instructions, and replace them sooner if they show any bulge, crack, or soft spot.
  • Leave slack and avoid kinks: pull the washer out, check that the hoses are not bent sharply, crushed against the wall, or stretched tight. A kinked hose wears through at the bend.
  • Turn the valves off when you travel: if you leave the house for a weekend or longer, close both washer valves. A hose cannot burst while the water is off.
  • Do not run the washer while you are out: a load started before work finishes while the house is empty. Run it when you are home, so a burst is found in minutes, not hours.
  • Put a drain pan under the washer: a pan with a drain line catches slow leaks and small bursts and sends the water to a drain instead of the floor. It helps most on second-floor laundry rooms, where a leak becomes a ceiling stain downstairs.
  • Consider a leak sensor: a small battery sensor on the laundry room floor sounds an alarm when it touches water. Some models also shut the water off automatically. It is a small purchase that buys you the minutes that matter most.

Also check the drain hose — the one that carries dirty water out of the washer into the standpipe. It is not under pressure, so it does not burst the same way, but it can slip out of the standpipe or crack with age, and the result looks the same: a flooded laundry room. Make sure it is seated firmly in the standpipe and secured so vibration cannot walk it out.

What Should You Do Next?

First, go and look at your hoses today. It takes two minutes: pull the washer forward a few inches, run your hand along each hose, and look for bulges, cracks, rust at the fittings, or a hose that feels soft and swollen. If anything looks wrong, replace the hoses before the next load of laundry.

Second, know where your washer shut-off valves are before you need them. In most Orlando homes they are on the wall behind the washer, behind a small access panel or recessed box. Turn each one once to check it still moves — valves that have sat untouched for years can seize, and a seized valve is no help during a burst.
