Kitchen Sink Leak in Orlando: Signs, Causes, and What to Do First

A kitchen sink leak usually starts as a slow drip inside a closed cabinet, and by the time you notice it the wood is already soft. Here are the signs to look for, the causes behind most leaks, and what to do first.

MV
Orlando Rapid Restoration Team Local Restoration Experts
• Published: November 12, 2026 • 7 min read • Orlando Technical Team

A kitchen sink leak is easy to ignore and expensive to leave alone. The leak usually starts small — a slow drip from a supply line, a loose drain connection, a faucet base that weeps a little water every time you run the tap. Because most of the plumbing sits inside a closed cabinet, you may not see the water at all until the cabinet floor is soft, the wood has swollen, or a musty smell has settled into the kitchen. By then the water has often spread further than the cabinet, into the subfloor and the wall behind the sink.

This guide explains how kitchen sink leaks start in Orlando homes, the signs to look for, what to do in the first hour, how drying and repair usually work, and how to stop the next leak before it starts. It applies to single-family homes, townhomes, and condos across Downtown Orlando, Winter Park, Lake Nona, College Park, Baldwin Park, and the rest of Orange County.

Why Is a Kitchen Sink Leak So Damaging?

A kitchen sink leak is damaging for a simple reason: the water hides. The cabinet under the sink is dark, closed, and full of cleaning supplies, so a slow leak can run for weeks before anyone opens the door and looks at the floor of the cabinet. In that time the water soaks into the cabinet base, which in most Orlando homes is particleboard or plywood. Particleboard swells and crumbles when it stays wet, and once it has swollen it does not go back to its original shape. The cabinet floor can sag, the doors can stop closing properly, and the damage spreads to the flooring in front of the sink.

Orlando's humidity makes a hidden leak worse. A damp cabinet in a humid kitchen dries slowly, and damp wood and drywall are where mold gets its start. Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours in Central Florida humidity, which means a leak that has been running quietly for a week has already had plenty of time to create a mold problem behind the cabinet and inside the wall. The water can also travel along the subfloor to the next room, so a leak under the kitchen sink sometimes shows up first as a stain on the ceiling of the room below, or as warped flooring in the dining area.

The other reason a sink leak causes so much damage is that the kitchen sink is used constantly. Every time the tap runs, the dishwasher drains, or the garbage disposal runs, water passes through the same fittings that are leaking. A leak that only drips when the tap is on still adds up to a large amount of water over days and weeks of normal kitchen use.

What Are the Signs of a Kitchen Sink Leak?

Most kitchen sink leaks announce themselves if you know where to look. Open the cabinet under the sink once a month and check for these signs:

  • A musty or damp smell when you open the cabinet: this is often the first sign, and it can appear before you see any water at all. A cabinet that smells damp is a cabinet that has been wet.
  • Water stains, dark spots, or discolouration on the cabinet floor: look for rings, streaks, or dark patches on the wood, and check the back wall of the cabinet too.
  • A soft, spongy, or swollen cabinet floor: press the floor of the cabinet with your hand. If it gives under pressure, feels soft, or has swollen upward, water has been soaking into it.
  • Peeling laminate or bubbling on the cabinet surfaces: moisture lifts the thin laminate layer off particleboard and makes paint bubble and peel.
  • Drips or wet spots on the supply lines, drain pipes, or disposal: run the tap and watch the connections. A drip at a joint, a wet nut, or water beading on a pipe is a leak, even if it is slow.
  • Warped or stained flooring in front of the sink: if the floorboards near the sink are cupping, the grout is darkening, or the baseboard is swelling, water has escaped the cabinet.
  • Your water bill creeping up without a clear reason: a supply-side leak that runs constantly will show up on the meter. A drain-side leak will not, because that water has already passed through the meter.

If you find any of these signs, do not wait to see whether the leak gets worse. A kitchen sink leak does not fix itself, and every day it runs is another day of water soaking into wood that is hard to dry once it is saturated. If the cabinet floor is already soft or the smell is strong, the damage has gone beyond a simple drip, and that is the point to get a professional assessment of what is wet behind the cabinet. What Usually Causes a Kitchen Sink to Leak?

Kitchen sink leaks come from a small set of familiar places. Knowing where to look saves time and keeps a small repair from turning into a cabinet replacement.

Supply lines. Two flexible lines, hot and cold, bring water to the faucet. In older Orlando homes these are often braided steel or plastic lines with compression fittings. The fittings loosen with vibration and age, the rubber washers inside them harden and crack, and the lines themselves can corrode at the connections. A supply-line leak runs whenever the water is on, so it is the kind of leak that can flood a cabinet overnight.

Drain and trap connections. The P-trap and the drain tailpiece carry wastewater away from the sink. These joints are held together by slip nuts that loosen over time, especially if the disposal vibrates or someone bumps the pipes while storing things in the cabinet. A drain leak only runs when water goes down the sink, which is why it can be hard to spot — the cabinet looks dry until you run the tap and watch.

The faucet base and the sink rim. The seal where the faucet meets the countertop and the seal where the sink meets the counter can wear out. Water from washing up runs along the countertop, under the faucet base, and down into the cabinet. The same thing happens when the caulk around the sink rim cracks or pulls away. This kind of leak leaves a stain on the cabinet ceiling, directly under the sink.

The garbage disposal. Disposals leak from the seal where they meet the sink flange, from the dishwasher connection hose, or from the body of the unit itself when the internal seals wear out. A disposal that is more than a few years old and has started to rust from the inside is a common source of slow cabinet leaks in Orlando kitchens.

The dishwasher connection. In most kitchens the dishwasher drains through the sink's plumbing, and its supply line often runs through the same cabinet. A loose dishwasher drain hose or a cracked supply line leaks into the cabinet every time the dishwasher runs, and because the dishwasher runs while nobody is in the kitchen, the leak can go unnoticed for a long time.

What Should You Do First When You Find a Kitchen Sink Leak?

Do the simple things first, in this order. Most kitchen sink leaks can be stopped in a few minutes once you know where the shut-off is.

  • Turn off the water at the sink shut-off valves: there are usually two small valves on the wall inside the cabinet, one hot and one cold. Turn both clockwise until they stop. If the leak is on the drain side, the valves will not stop it, but they remove the supply pressure while you work.
  • Empty the cabinet and mop up the standing water: take everything out, towel up the water on the cabinet floor, and put a dry towel or a shallow tray under the drip so you can see whether it is still leaking.
  • Find the source: dry the pipes with a towel, then run the tap and watch each joint. Check the supply lines, the trap, the disposal, and the faucet base, one at a time, so you know which connection is failing.
  • Tighten a loose slip nut by hand: if a drain joint is dripping because the nut has worked loose, a gentle hand-tighten sometimes stops it. Do not force it with pliers — overtightening cracks plastic traps and makes the leak worse.
  • Take photos and video: film the leak, the wet cabinet floor, and any swollen wood or staining, with timestamps. Insurers and contractors both want to see the damage as you found it.
  • Call your insurer the same day if the damage is more than a damp cabinet floor: a sudden supply-line failure is usually treated as a sudden plumbing failure, which is the kind of water damage most homeowners policies are written to cover. Read the insurance section below before you assume anything.

If the water has spread past the cabinet — into the subfloor, the wall behind the sink, or the room below — that is the point to call a restoration professional rather than handling it with towels. The drying equipment used after a kitchen leak, like air movers and dehumidifiers, is not the same as a household fan, and moisture readings tell you when the cabinet and wall are actually dry instead of just looking dry. What Does Drying Under a Kitchen Sink Involve?

Real drying after a kitchen sink leak is not mopping the cabinet and leaving the door open. Water gets into the cabinet base, the subfloor underneath it, and the wall cavity behind the sink, and those materials dry slowly because air cannot reach them. A drying job usually starts with moisture readings — a meter pressed against the cabinet floor, the baseboards, and the wall to map how far the water spread and how wet each material is. Those readings are taken again over the following days, so the drying stops when the materials reach their normal moisture level, not when the surface feels dry to the touch.

The equipment is straightforward: air movers push dry air across wet surfaces, and dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room's air so the surfaces can keep releasing water. In a kitchen this often means pulling the cabinet base out or drilling small holes in the cabinet floor so air can reach the subfloor below, and sometimes removing a strip of drywall behind the sink so air can reach the wall cavity. Flooring in front of the sink may need to come up if water got underneath it — vinyl plank and laminate trap water below them, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the top looks fine.

How long drying takes depends on how much water escaped, which materials got wet, and how quickly the leak was found. A small drip caught in a day dries much faster than a leak that ran for weeks. The honest answer is that nobody can give you a drying time until the moisture readings are in hand. If you want a sense of the cost side of the same job, see our guide to how much water extraction costs in Orlando.

One thing worth knowing: drying and repair are two separate jobs. Drying removes the water and stops mold from taking hold. Repair replaces the cabinet base, drywall, and flooring that could not be saved. A contractor who skips the drying step and goes straight to a new cabinet base is building over wet material, and the problems — odour, swelling, mould — show up weeks later.

Will Homeowners Insurance Cover a Kitchen Sink Leak?

It depends on how the leak started, and insurers draw a sharp line between sudden and gradual damage. A supply line that bursts without warning is usually treated as a sudden and accidental water discharge, which is the kind of water damage a standard Florida homeowners policy is usually written to cover. A drain connection that has been dripping slowly for months while the cabinet rotted is often treated as a maintenance problem and denied.

That distinction is why your photos, the date you found the water, and the failed part matter so much. They are what show the insurer the leak was sudden. Keep the failed hose or fitting, keep the photos, and file the claim promptly — most policies ask you to report damage quickly, and delays give an adjuster room to argue the damage grew because you waited.

Your deductible still applies, so a small leak that only dampens the cabinet floor may cost less to fix than the deductible itself, in which case filing a claim may not be worth it. Larger leaks — water through the subfloor, soaked drywall, damaged cabinets — are where the claim earns its keep. How Do You Keep a Kitchen Sink From Leaking Again?

Prevention here is cheap, simple, and mostly a matter of looking inside the cabinet once in a while. Put a reminder on your calendar and check the plumbing every few months:

  • Look inside the cabinet every month: open the door, run the tap, and watch the connections for a minute. A leak caught in its first week is a towel job; a leak caught in its third month is a cabinet job.
  • Replace old supply lines before they fail: if the lines under your sink are plain rubber or old plastic, replace them with braided stainless steel lines. Check the fittings for rust or green corrosion and replace the line if the braid is frayed or kinked.
  • Do not overload the cabinet: heavy bottles and cleaning supplies stacked against the drain pipes bump the slip nuts loose over time. Keep the area around the pipes clear.
  • Re-caulk the sink rim and faucet base when the caulk cracks: a bead of fresh silicone around the sink rim and the faucet base keeps wash-up water on the countertop, where it belongs.
  • Run the garbage disposal with cold water: cold water keeps grease solid so the disposal can grind it and flush it away, instead of letting it coat the drain pipes and build up into a clog that backs water up into the cabinet.
  • Check the dishwasher hose when the dishwasher runs: open the cabinet during a dishwasher cycle once in a while and watch the drain hose connection for drips.
  • Put a small leak sensor under the sink: a battery sensor on the cabinet floor sounds an alarm when it touches water. It is a small purchase that buys you the days that matter most with a slow leak.

Also check the shut-off valves themselves. Turn each one once or twice a year to check it still moves — valves that have sat untouched for years can seize, and a seized valve is no help when a supply line starts spraying.

What Should You Do Next?

First, go and open the cabinet under your kitchen sink today. It takes two minutes: run the tap, watch the connections, press the cabinet floor, and sniff for damp. If anything is wet, swollen, or smelly, find the source before the next load of dishes.

Second, know where your sink shut-off valves are before you need them. In most Orlando homes they are on the wall inside the cabinet, behind the cleaning supplies. Turn each one once to check it still moves.
