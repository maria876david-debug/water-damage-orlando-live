A wet ceiling in a condo is never just a leak. In a single-family home the question is simple: it is your house, so it is your repair. In a condo, three parties can be involved — you, the neighbor above you, and the homeowners association — and each one has a different idea of whose problem it is.

Orlando has thousands of condo units, from high-rises in Downtown Orlando to townhome-style communities in Lake Nona, Baldwin Park, and Winter Park. The buildings share walls, pipes, and roofs, so water rarely respects unit boundaries. This guide explains how responsibility is usually divided, what to document, and what to do first when you find water in your condo.

Who Is Responsible for Water Damage in a Florida Condo?

There is no single rule that covers every condo building. Responsibility usually depends on two things: where the water came from, and what your association's declaration says. The declaration is the governing document for your community, and it draws the line between what the association owns and what you own. That line is different in almost every building, so read your own declaration first after a leak.

As a general pattern in Florida condos, the association is usually responsible for common elements — the roof, exterior walls, hallways, and shared plumbing that serves more than one unit. The unit owner is usually responsible for everything inside their own unit, including fixtures, appliances, and interior finishes. When water crosses from one area to another, the source of the leak usually decides who pays to fix the source, while each owner typically handles the damage inside their own unit through their own insurance.

That surprises many owners. Even when a neighbor's pipe caused your ceiling stain, your own insurance often pays for your unit's damage first, and the insurers sort out fault between themselves afterwards.

What Does the HOA Usually Cover — and What Is Yours?

The table below shows how responsibility usually falls in the most common Orlando condo scenarios. These are typical divisions, not guarantees — your declaration can shift any of them.

Who Usually Pays for Common Condo Water Damage

Scenario Usually responsible Why
Roof leak dripping into a top-floor unit The association The roof is a common element
Shared drain stack or riser bursts The association Shared plumbing serves multiple units
Pipe bursts inside your unit's walls The unit owner The pipe serves only your unit
Your washing machine hose or dishwasher leaks The unit owner Your appliance, your supply lines
Neighbor's overflowing tub damages your ceiling The neighbor, through their liability coverage The source was inside their unit
Hallway or common-area pipe leaks into units The association Common-area plumbing

One grey area worth knowing about is the association's insurance deductible. Many Florida condo associations carry a large deductible on their master policy, and in some communities the declaration lets the association pass part of that deductible to the owners affected by a claim. Ask your property manager how your building handles deductibles before you need the answer.

What If the Leak Came From the Unit Above You?

A leak from the unit above is the most common condo water damage dispute in Orlando, and it is also the one where timing matters most. Water does not wait for a committee meeting. Tell your neighbor and your property manager or HOA the same day, in writing — a text message or email counts, and it creates a record of when you reported it.

Do not go into the neighbor's unit yourself, and do not try to fix their plumbing. If the neighbor is away or will not answer, the property manager or association can usually arrange access for an emergency shut-off. Your job is to protect your own unit: move valuables away from the wet area, take photos, and get the water out of your space.

Be ready for the neighbor to deny responsibility. That is normal, and it is not a reason to delay drying your own unit: mold can begin growing on wet drywall within 24 to 48 hours in Orlando's humidity. Dry first, argue about the bill with the documentation in hand.

What Should You Document Before Anyone Starts Repairs?

Documentation is what turns a disagreement into a payable claim. Before anyone cuts drywall or pulls up flooring, record the damage the way an adjuster would want to see it:

  • Photos and video with timestamps: film every wet room, the ceiling stain, warped flooring, and the suspected source of the leak if it is visible and safe to approach.
  • The time you found it: write down the date and time you discovered the water.
  • Who you notified, and when: keep the names, times, and messages for your neighbor, property manager, HOA, and insurer.
  • Written notes from anyone who inspects it: ask the plumber or the contractor who looks at the leak to put the apparent source in writing.
  • Receipts for everything you spend: fans, dehumidifier rental, tarps, and any emergency supplies, with dates.
  • Damaged items: photograph rugs, furniture, and electronics in place before anything is thrown away.

Keep it all in one folder. When the HOA, your insurer, or the neighbor's insurer asks what happened, a dated folder answers faster than memory.

What Does Condo Insurance (HO-6) Actually Cover?

A condo unit owner's policy, usually called HO-6, generally covers the interior of your unit — flooring, cabinets, and personal property — plus liability if water from your unit damages someone else's. The association's master policy generally covers the building itself: the structure, the roof, and the common elements. The gap between the two is where most owners get caught out.

Common gaps include improvements you made to the unit, like upgraded flooring or a renovated kitchen, which the master policy may not cover at full value. Another is the association's deductible, which can be large enough that its policy pays nothing on a moderate claim. Read your HO-6 policy and your declaration together before you need them.

For a fuller explanation of how homeowners insurance treats water damage in Florida, see our guide to homeowners insurance and water damage in Florida. The rules for single-family homes and condos differ, but the documentation habits are the same.

What Should You Do First When You Find Water in Your Condo?

First, stop the water if you can do it safely. If the leak is inside your unit, turn off your unit's water shut-off valve, usually near the water heater or under the kitchen sink. If the water is coming through a wall or ceiling and you cannot find a valve, call your property manager or HOA emergency line and ask them to shut off the building supply for your stack. Do not touch electrical fixtures or outlets that have been in contact with water.

Second, move what you can save and start the documentation from the section above. Third, notify your HOA or property manager and your insurer the same day.
