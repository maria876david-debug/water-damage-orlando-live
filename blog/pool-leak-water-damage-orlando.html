Pool Leak Water Damage in Orlando: Signs Your Pool Is Damaging Your Home

A pool leak does not stay in the pool. It washes away soil, cracks the deck, and can reach your foundation and flooring. Here are the signs to watch for and what to do first.

MV
Orlando Rapid Restoration Team Local Restoration Experts
• Published: November 12, 2026 • 7 min read • Orlando Technical Team

Orlando is a pool town. Most homes in the suburbs have one, and in a climate where the pool runs most of the year, a leak is not a rare event — it is a maintenance problem that every pool owner faces sooner or later. The trouble is that a pool leak does not always look like a pool problem. The water escapes underground, soaks the soil around the shell, and can undermine the deck, settle the patio, and work its way toward the house. By the time the damage shows up inside, the leak has often been running for weeks.

This guide explains how pool leaks damage Orlando homes, the signs to watch for, what to do when you suspect one, how repair and drying usually work when pool water reaches the house, and how to keep it from happening again. It applies to in-ground pools across Downtown Orlando, Winter Park, Lake Nona, College Park, Baldwin Park, and the rest of Orange County.

How Does a Pool Leak Damage More Than the Pool?

A pool holds a large volume of water, and a leak lets that water out into the ground around it. The damage starts with the soil. Central Florida soil is sandy, and sandy soil washes away easily when water flows through it. A steady leak can hollow out the ground under the pool deck, and the deck then cracks, sinks, or tilts toward the pool. The same thing can happen to the patio and the walkways around the pool, and to the ground under the pool equipment pad.

The water can also travel. It follows the path of least resistance through the soil, and if the pool sits close to the house — as many Orlando pools do — the water can reach the foundation and the slab. A slab that stays wet from one side can wick moisture up through the concrete and into the flooring inside, which is how a pool leak sometimes shows up as damp carpet, cupped wood, or a musty smell in the room nearest the pool. In a home with a raised foundation, the water can pool under the house and keep the subfloor damp for months.

There is also the water itself. Pool water is chlorinated, and chlorinated water is harder on building materials than plain rainwater. It stains, it corrodes metal fittings and rebar, and it leaves salt and mineral deposits behind as it dries. A leak that reaches the house is not just a water problem — it is a water problem that carries chemicals with it, and the materials it touches need to be dried and cleaned properly, not just left to air out.

Finally, a pool leak costs money even before it damages anything. A pool that loses water needs topping up, and the pump runs longer to keep the level right. If the leak is in the plumbing, the pump can also lose prime and run dry, which damages the pump itself. A leak found early is a repair; a leak found late is a repair plus a deck, a patio, and a floor.

What Are the Signs Your Pool Is Leaking?

Pools lose some water to evaporation, especially in Orlando's heat, so a dropping water level on its own is not proof of a leak. The signs below, taken together, are what separate normal evaporation from a real leak:

  • The water level drops faster than evaporation can explain: mark the water level with tape and check it again after a day with the pump off. If it keeps dropping with the pump off, the leak is in the shell or the fittings. If it only drops with the pump on, the leak is usually in the plumbing.
  • Wet spots, soggy grass, or mud near the pool when it has not rained: a patch of ground that stays wet, or grass that grows faster and greener in one spot, often sits over a leaking pipe.
  • Cracks, sinking, or tilting in the pool deck: a deck that has settled or cracked in one area is often sitting over soil that a leak has washed away underneath.
  • Air bubbles in the pump basket or a pump that loses prime: a suction-side leak lets air into the plumbing, and the pump struggles to hold its prime. Bubbles in the pump basket are a classic sign.
  • Your water bill climbing without a clear reason: topping up a leaking pool adds up, and a steady rise in the bill is often the first sign a homeowner notices.
  • Damp flooring, a musty smell, or staining in the room nearest the pool: if pool water has reached the house, it shows up the same way any other leak does — damp floors, swollen baseboards, and a musty smell that does not go away.
  • Algae that keeps coming back no matter how much you treat the water: a leak dilutes the chemicals as fast as you add them, so the water never quite holds its balance.

If you see more than one of these signs, do the bucket test: place a bucket of pool water on a pool step so the water inside the bucket sits at the same level as the pool, mark both levels, and compare them after a day. If the pool drops more than the bucket, water is leaving the pool somewhere other than evaporation. That is the point to call a pool leak specialist for a proper test. Where Do Orlando Pools Usually Leak?

Pool leaks come from a small set of familiar places. Knowing where to look saves time and keeps a small repair from turning into a deck replacement.

The skimmer and the returns. The skimmer is where the pool's plumbing meets the shell, and the joint between the skimmer throat and the concrete is a common leak point. The return fittings, where clean water flows back into the pool, can also loosen or crack. These are the first places a leak test usually checks.

The main drain. The drain at the deep end sits under the most water pressure in the pool, and its seal and plumbing are hard to reach. A main drain leak is often the hardest to find and the most disruptive to fix, because it is at the bottom of the pool.

The light niche. The underwater light sits in a niche cut into the shell, and the conduit that carries its cable runs back to the equipment pad. Water can leak through the niche seal or travel along the conduit, and a light-niche leak often shows up as a wet spot in the yard between the pool and the house.

Underground plumbing. The pipes that run from the pool to the pump, filter, and heater sit underground, and they move with the soil. In Central Florida the sandy soil shifts, roots grow into the lines, and older PVC can crack at the joints. A plumbing leak usually shows up as wet ground, a pump that loses prime, or a water bill that keeps climbing.

The shell itself. Cracks in plaster, marcite, or tile grout let water through, and in an older pool the shell can develop structural cracks as the ground settles. Small surface cracks are often cosmetic, but a crack that runs through the shell and keeps growing is a leak that needs a professional look.

What Should You Do When You Suspect a Pool Leak?

Do the simple things first, in this order. Most of the diagnosis can be done without draining the pool.

  • Do the bucket test: as described above, it separates evaporation from a real leak in a day, and it costs nothing.
  • Check the equipment pad: look at the pump, filter, heater, and valves for drips, wet spots, or white mineral deposits. A leak at the pad is the easiest kind to find and fix.
  • Watch the water level with the pump on and off: if the level only drops while the pump runs, the leak is in the pressure side of the plumbing. If it drops with the pump off too, the leak is in the shell, the skimmer, or the suction side.
  • Look at the deck and the yard: walk the deck and the ground between the pool and the house. Soft spots, sinking pavers, and wet patches that never dry are clues to where the water is going.
  • Do not drain the pool to look for the leak: an empty in-ground pool in Florida can lift or crack as groundwater pushes up from below, and draining it can cause far more damage than the leak. Leave the water in and let a specialist test it.
  • Take photos and video: film the water level, the wet spots, the deck cracks, and any damp flooring inside the house, with timestamps. Insurers and contractors both want to see the damage as you found it.

If the water has reached the house — damp flooring, swollen baseboards, or a musty smell in the room nearest the pool — that is the point to call a restoration professional as well as a pool specialist. The pool leak needs a pool repair, but the water inside the house needs drying, and the two jobs run in parallel. What Do Repair and Drying Involve When Pool Water Reaches the House?

Fixing a pool leak and fixing the damage it caused inside the house are two different jobs, and they usually happen at the same time. The pool side starts with a leak test — dye testing around fittings, pressure testing the lines, and listening for the leak with acoustic equipment — so the repair targets the right place instead of guessing. A skimmer or return leak can often be sealed without draining the pool. A plumbing leak usually means digging up the line and replacing the cracked section. A shell crack may need patching, replastering, or a structural repair, depending on how deep it runs.

The house side starts with moisture readings — a meter pressed against the flooring, the baseboards, and the wall nearest the pool to map how far the water spread and how wet each material is. Those readings are taken again over the following days, so the drying stops when the materials reach their normal moisture level, not when the surface feels dry to the touch.

The equipment is straightforward: air movers push dry air across wet surfaces, and dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room's air so the surfaces can keep releasing water. In a home where pool water has reached the slab, this often means removing wet baseboards and lifting flooring so air can reach the subfloor, and sometimes cutting a strip of drywall at the bottom of the wall so air can reach the cavity. Pool water leaves chlorine and mineral deposits behind as it dries, so the affected materials need to be cleaned as well as dried.

How long drying takes depends on how much water escaped, which materials got wet, and how quickly the leak was found. A small leak caught in a week dries much faster than one that ran for a season. The honest answer is that nobody can give you a drying time until the moisture readings are in hand. If you want a sense of the cost side of the same job, see our guide to how much water extraction costs in Orlando.

One thing worth knowing: drying and repair are two separate jobs. Drying removes the water and stops mold from taking hold. Repair replaces the flooring, baseboards, and drywall that could not be saved. A contractor who skips the drying step and goes straight to new flooring is building over wet material, and the problems — odour, swelling, mould — show up weeks later.

Will Insurance Cover Pool Leak Water Damage?

It depends on how the leak started, what it damaged, and what your policy says about pools. A sudden pipe break in the pool plumbing is usually treated as a sudden and accidental water discharge, which is the kind of water damage a standard Florida homeowners policy is usually written to cover. A slow leak from a cracked shell or worn fittings that has been running for months is often treated as a maintenance problem and denied.

Pool structures themselves are often covered differently from the house. Some policies cover the pool shell and equipment, some cover only the house, and some exclude pools entirely or cover them only under a separate endorsement. Read the pool section of your own policy before you assume anything, and check whether the policy covers the cost of finding the leak — leak detection itself is sometimes excluded even when the damage is covered.

Your deductible still applies, and the water damage inside the house and the pool repair are often treated as separate claims with separate deductibles. That is why your photos, the date you found the water, and the failed part matter so much — they are what show the insurer the leak was sudden. Keep the photos, keep the repair invoices, and file the claim promptly. How Do You Keep a Pool From Leaking Again?

Prevention here is mostly a matter of watching the water level and the equipment pad, and catching small problems before they become big ones:

  • Mark the water level and check it weekly: a piece of tape on the skimmer or the tile line takes ten seconds to check, and a level that keeps dropping is the earliest warning you will get.
  • Walk the equipment pad once a month: look for drips, wet spots, and white mineral deposits on the pump, filter, heater, and valves. A drip at the pad is a cheap fix today and a plumbing repair tomorrow.
  • Keep the water chemistry balanced: water that is too acidic eats at plaster, grout, and fittings, and water that is too alkaline leaves scale that cracks and clogs. Balanced water protects the shell as well as the swimmers.
  • Watch the deck for settling and cracks: a deck that is sinking or cracking in one spot is often sitting over soil that a leak has washed away. Catch it early and the fix is a pipe, not a deck.
  • Keep trees and roots away from the lines: roots seek water, and a root that finds a pool line will crack it. If a tree is growing over the plumbing run, it is worth having the lines checked.
  • Have the pool inspected when you buy a home: a pool inspection, including a pressure test of the lines, is cheap compared with a leak that has been running for a year before you move in.
  • Do not let the pool sit empty: an empty in-ground pool in Florida can lift or crack as groundwater pushes up from below. If the pool needs work, keep water in it until the repair starts.

Also keep an eye on the ground between the pool and the house after heavy rain. A wet patch that never dries, even days after the rain has stopped, is worth a closer look.

What Should You Do Next?

First, go and mark your pool's water level today. It takes a minute: a piece of tape on the tile line, a note of the date, and a check tomorrow. If the level is dropping faster than evaporation can explain, do the bucket test.

Second, walk the deck and the ground between the pool and the house, and look at the equipment pad for drips. The signs of a pool leak are usually visible if you know where to look.
