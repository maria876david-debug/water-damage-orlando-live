Hardwood Floor Water Damage in Orlando: Repair or Replace?

Wood swells, cups, and buckles when it gets wet, and a floor that looks fine on top can still be wet underneath. Here is how to read the damage and how the repair-or-replace decision is made.

MV
Orlando Rapid Restoration Team Local Restoration Experts
• Published: November 12, 2026 • 8 min read • Orlando Technical Team

Hardwood floors are one of the most popular choices in Orlando homes, and one of the least forgiving when water gets on them. Wood is a natural material that absorbs water, swells, and changes shape — and once a board has swollen and dried in a new shape, it rarely goes back to the way it was. A leak that would be a mop job on tile can be a floor replacement on hardwood, which is why the first hours after water hits a wood floor matter so much.

This guide explains why hardwood reacts the way it does to water, the signs of damage to look for, what to do in the first hours, how the repair-or-replace decision is actually made, and how to protect your floors in future. It applies to solid hardwood, engineered wood, and laminate floors in homes across Downtown Orlando, Winter Park, Lake Nona, College Park, Baldwin Park, and the rest of Orange County.

Why Does Hardwood React So Badly to Water?

Wood and water do not mix, and the reason is built into the material itself. Wood is made of tiny fibres that soak up water like a sponge. When a board absorbs water, the fibres swell and the board gets wider and thicker. Because the board is nailed or glued down and has nowhere to expand, the swelling pushes the edges upward — which is why a wet hardwood floor cups, crowns, and buckles instead of just looking damp.

The finish on top of the floor slows the water down but does not stop it. Water gets in through the seams between boards, through scratches in the finish, and from underneath, where there is no finish at all. Once water is under the floor, it soaks into the subfloor too, and the subfloor swells and holds the moisture against the boards from below. That is why a floor can look dry on top while the wood underneath is still wet.

Orlando's humidity makes the problem worse in two ways. First, a wet floor in a humid house dries slowly, so the wood stays swollen longer and has more time to warp permanently. Second, damp wood and a damp subfloor are where mold gets its start. Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours in Central Florida humidity, which means a floor that has been wet for a weekend already needs more than a mop — it needs drying and a check for what is growing underneath.

The other reason hardwood is so unforgiving is that the damage is visible and permanent. A swollen board does not shrink back to flat when it dries. It may settle a little, but the cupping and the gaps usually stay, and the finish often cracks and peels where the board moved. A floor that has been through a serious soaking rarely looks the way it did before, even after a good drying job.

What Are the Signs of Water Damage in a Hardwood Floor?

Water damage in a hardwood floor shows up in the shape of the boards as much as in their colour. Walk the floor slowly and look for these signs:

  • Cupping: the edges of the boards are higher than the centres, so the floor feels wavy underfoot. This is the most common sign, and it means the underside of the boards is wetter than the top.
  • Crowning: the centres of the boards are higher than the edges — the opposite of cupping. Crowning often appears after a cupped floor has been sanded too soon, before the wood had finished drying.
  • Buckling: boards have lifted off the subfloor entirely, sometimes by a noticeable amount. Buckling means the floor has expanded so much that it has nowhere left to go, and it is the most serious shape change a wood floor can show.
  • Gaps between boards: boards that have shrunk after drying leave wide gaps, and boards that have swollen can crush together and then shrink back unevenly.
  • Dark stains and discolouration: water leaves grey, black, or dark brown stains in the wood, especially where it has sat for a while. Black stains often mean the water has reached the tannins in the wood or that mold is growing in the grain.
  • A soft or spongy feeling underfoot: if the floor gives or feels soft in one area, the subfloor underneath is wet or has started to rot.
  • Peeling, flaking, or cloudy finish: the finish lifts, turns white or cloudy, and flakes off where water has sat on it.
  • A musty smell that will not go away: a damp smell in a room with wood floors usually means water is trapped under the boards, even if the surface looks dry.

If you see cupping or buckling, do not wait to see whether the floor settles on its own. Cupping can improve as the floor dries, but buckling and black staining usually mean the boards are already damaged. If the floor feels soft underfoot, the subfloor is involved too, and that is the point to get a professional assessment with moisture readings. Where Does the Water Usually Come From?

Hardwood floors rarely get wet on their own. The water almost always comes from somewhere else in the house, and finding the source matters as much as drying the floor — a floor dried over a leak that is still running will be wet again in a week.

Appliance leaks. The most common sources in Orlando kitchens are the dishwasher, the refrigerator water line, and the washing machine. All three sit on or near wood floors, and all three leak slowly and quietly. A dishwasher that seeps a little water with every cycle can soak the floor in front of it for months before anyone notices the cupping.

Plumbing leaks. A supply line, a drain connection, or a toilet seal that leaks will send water under the floor, where it spreads along the subfloor and shows up as cupping a room away from the leak itself. Our guide to the signs of hidden water damage in walls covers the same kind of slow, out-of-sight leak.

Roof and window leaks. A roof leak or a window that lets rain in during a storm can soak a wood floor in one spot, and the damage often shows up as a dark stain and cupping near the window or under the leak.

Air conditioning condensation. In Orlando's humidity, an air handler or a clogged condensate drain can drip water onto the floor for weeks. The AC drain line is a common culprit — see our guide to AC drain line clogs and water damage.

Flooding and storm water. Heavy rain, a backed-up drain, or a burst pipe can put standing water on a wood floor in minutes. Standing water is the worst case for hardwood, because the boards soak from the top and the bottom at the same time.

Everyday spills left too long. A spilled drink mopped up straight away is harmless. A plant pot that leaks a little water every time it is watered, or a pet bowl that splashes daily, will stain and cup the boards underneath it over time.

What Should You Do First When Your Hardwood Floor Gets Wet?

Do the simple things first, in this order. Speed matters with hardwood, because the longer the boards stay wet, the more they swell and the less likely they are to return to flat.

  • Stop the water at its source: turn off the appliance, close the shut-off valve, or stop the leak before you start mopping. Drying a floor over a leak that is still running wastes the effort.
  • Remove the standing water: towels, a mop, and a wet-dry vacuum will move the water. Work from the edges of the wet area toward the centre so you are not pushing water further under the boards.
  • Move furniture and rugs off the wet floor: rugs trap moisture against the wood, and furniture legs leave stains and dents in softened boards. Lift what you can and get air to the floor.
  • Get air moving, but do not use heat: open windows and run fans across the floor. Do not use a heater or a heat gun on the boards — heat dries the top faster than the bottom, which makes cupping worse.
  • Do not sand a wet floor: sanding cupped boards while they are still wet removes the high edges, and when the boards dry and settle, the sanded spots end up low. The floor needs to be dry before anyone sands it.
  • Take photos and video: film the water, the cupping, the stains, and the source of the leak, with timestamps. Insurers and contractors both want to see the damage as you found it.

If the water has spread across a large area, soaked through to the subfloor, or the boards are already buckling, that is the point to call a restoration professional rather than handling it with towels. The drying equipment used after a floor soaking, like air movers and dehumidifiers, is not the same as a household fan, and moisture readings tell you when the boards and the subfloor are actually dry instead of just looking dry. Repair or Replace? How the Decision Is Made

The repair-or-replace question is the one every homeowner with a wet wood floor asks, and the honest answer is that it is decided by measurements, not by looking at the floor. Here is how a professional usually works through it.

Step one is drying, and drying comes before any decision. The floor is dried with air movers and dehumidifiers, and moisture readings are taken in the boards and the subfloor over several days. A floor that looks badly cupped when wet can settle a surprising amount as it dries, and a floor that looks fine on top can still be wet underneath. No repair decision is sound until the readings show the wood is back to its normal moisture level.

Step two is seeing what is left after drying. Once the floor is dry, the cupping, gaps, and stains that remain are the real damage. Light cupping sometimes flattens out on its own as the boards dry evenly. Moderate cupping can often be sanded flat and refinished, as long as the boards are thick enough to take a sanding and the finish has not failed completely. Solid hardwood can usually be sanded and refinished more than once; engineered wood has a thin top layer and can only be sanded a limited number of times, and laminate cannot be sanded at all.

Step three is checking the subfloor. If the subfloor is soft, swollen, or stained, the boards above it will never sit flat, no matter how much sanding is done. A damaged subfloor has to be replaced, and that means the boards above it come up too. This is the point where a repair turns into a replacement for that section of floor.

Replacement is usually the answer when: boards are buckled and have lifted off the subfloor, the wood is black-stained through the grain, the boards are soft or crumbling, the finish has failed across a large area, or the floor is laminate or thin engineered wood that cannot be refinished. Replacement can also be the answer when the damaged boards cannot be matched — a patch of new boards next to old ones can look worse than a new floor, and a good contractor will say so plainly.

Repair is usually the answer when: the cupping is light and the boards settle as they dry, the stains are shallow and sand out, the subfloor is sound, and the floor is solid hardwood with enough thickness left to refinish. A repair in this case usually means sanding the floor flat, refinishing it, and replacing only the boards that could not be saved.

One thing worth knowing: drying and repair are two separate jobs, and the drying has to finish before the repair starts. A contractor who sands and refinishes a floor that is still wet is building over wet material, and the cupping comes back as the wood finishes drying underneath the new finish. Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Hardwood Floor Water Damage?

It depends on how the water got there, and insurers draw a sharp line between sudden and gradual damage. A burst pipe or a supply line that fails without warning is usually treated as a sudden and accidental water discharge, which is the kind of water damage a standard Florida homeowners policy is usually written to cover. A dishwasher that has been seeping slowly for months while the floor cupped is often treated as a maintenance problem and denied.

That distinction is why your photos, the date you found the water, and the source of the leak matter so much. They are what show the insurer the damage was sudden. Keep the failed part, keep the photos, and file the claim promptly — most policies ask you to report damage quickly, and delays give an adjuster room to argue the damage grew because you waited.

Your deductible still applies, so a small area of cupping may cost less to fix than the deductible itself, in which case filing a claim may not be worth it. Larger damage — a whole room of buckled boards, a ruined subfloor — is where the claim earns its keep. Note that insurers usually pay for the damaged area, not for refinishing the whole floor to match, so a patch that does not match the rest of the room is a common point of disagreement. How Do You Protect a Hardwood Floor From Water?

Protecting a hardwood floor is mostly about keeping water off it and catching leaks early:

  • Wipe up spills straight away: a spill mopped up in minutes is harmless. A spill left overnight soaks into the seams.
  • Put mats and trays where water lands: a mat in front of the sink, a tray under plant pots, and a mat under the pet bowls keep the daily drips off the wood.
  • Keep the finish in good shape: a worn finish lets water into the wood. If water stops beading on the surface and starts soaking in, the floor is due for a recoat.
  • Check the appliances that sit on or near wood floors: look behind the fridge and under the dishwasher every few months, and check the washing machine hoses the same way.
  • Keep the AC drain clear: a clogged condensate drain is one of the most common slow leaks in Orlando homes, and it often drips onto wood floors.
  • Watch the humidity inside the house: wood swells in humid air and shrinks in dry air. A dehumidifier in the damp months helps the floor stay stable, and it helps the house dry faster after any leak.
  • Put a small leak sensor near the dishwasher and the fridge: a battery sensor on the floor sounds an alarm when it touches water. It is a small purchase that buys you the hours that matter most with a wood floor.

Also remember that area rugs are not protection. A rug over a wet floor traps the moisture against the wood and hides the cupping until it is too late to fix. Lift rugs after any leak and let the floor dry in the open air.

What Should You Do Next?

First, go and walk your wood floors today. Look for cupping, gaps, dark stains, and soft spots, and check the boards in front of the sink, the dishwasher, and the fridge. If anything has changed shape, find the water source before the next load of dishes.

Second, do not sand a wet floor. Let it dry fully, get moisture readings if the area is large, and only then decide whether the boards can be saved.
